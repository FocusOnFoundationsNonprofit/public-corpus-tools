QUESTION: What are the recommendations for external controls for COVID-19 tests, specifically regarding their use in different settings such as point of care, CLIA high complexity, and moderate complexity labs, as well as for molecular tests intended for at-home testing?
ANSWER: For point of care and CLIA high and moderate complexity labs, the FDA recommends using external positive controls that are approximately five times the limit of detection (LOD) of the test to verify its performance. Controls recommended or included in the test's labeling should be validated within the analytical and clinical studies of the device. For at-home molecular tests, an external control is generally not expected, but an internal control is. Third-party external controls labeled for IVD use and recommended for use with a test but not included with the test kits do not need to be brought under the EUA holder's quality system.

QUESTION: Are manufacturers who received Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) early in the pandemic required to perform additional validation based on updated recommendations, such as evaluating each upper respiratory sample type as recommended in the October 6, 2021, molecular EUA template?
ANSWER: Updated recommendations in the EUA templates do not retroactively affect previously issued EUAs unless those authorizations are specifically revised or revoked. The validity of previous EUAs remains intact as initially issued. The FDA continues to monitor test performance post-authorization and will directly contact EUA holders if further evaluation is deemed necessary.

QUESTION: Will the FDA accept new EUAs for combination COVID-19 and flu A and B tests submitted after the 60-day window mentioned in the updated guidance?
ANSWER: The 45-day and 60-day time frames mentioned in the updated guidance refer to tests already being offered before submitting an EUA, guiding how long such tests can continue to be offered while the FDA reviews the EUA request. These time frames do not limit when new tests (those not offered prior to November 15, 2021) must be submitted. The FDA will continue to accept EUA requests for COVID-19 tests, including new tests, and will prioritize reviews based on the criteria outlined in the guidance update from November 15, 2021.

QUESTION: Can developers use existing real-time stability data from an EUA authorized point-of-care (POC) antigen test to support an EUA request for over-the-counter use for the same test? Additionally, is stability required for different kit size configurations?
ANSWER: Yes, if the over-the-counter version of the test is identical to the point-of-care version, leveraging the same stability data is acceptable. Developers should provide a side-by-side comparison to demonstrate that any differences between the test kits would not significantly affect stability. Generally, the size of the test kit is not expected to impact its stability, so different kit size configurations should not require separate stability data.

QUESTION: For submitting an OTC (over-the-counter) antigen test with two different swab manufacturers due to supply chain issues, how should equivalency testing be designed to ensure both swabs are acceptable for use with the test?
ANSWER: If the swabs are made of the same material and have the same dimensions, they may be considered interchangeable, and detailed comparisons should be provided in the submission. A bench experiment to confirm that the alternate swab performs well with the test may be sufficient, rather than a full clinical study. It's important to verify the materials and dimensions of the swabs and include this information in the submission. If there are significant differences between the swabs, such as material type changes (e.g., from spun polyester to a foam swab), a limit of detection (LOD) study or other bench studies may be necessary to establish similar performance. The safest approach is to detail the specifications of both swabs and reach out directly to the FDA via the inbox with these specific questions for further guidance.

QUESTION: After initially notifying the FDA of a validated IVD serology assay as an LDT and later validating the assay against the nucleocapsid protein to potentially aid in distinguishing immune response to prior infection versus vaccination, should a separate EUA be submitted for the nucleocapsid and RBD (receptor-binding domain) serology assays?
ANSWER: If you intend to offer both the nucleocapsid and RBD serology assays, then two separate EUA requests should be submitted, one for each test. However, if you decide to only offer the nucleocapsid assay and no longer plan to offer the RBD assay, you can request to be removed from the notification list for the RBD assay and then submit an EUA request for the nucleocapsid assay.

QUESTION: Regarding the FDA's home use template from November 9th that requires enrolling at least 10 positive individuals without symptoms or epidemiological reasons to suspect COVID-19 for an asymptomatic claim, what exactly constitutes "other epidemiological reasons to suspect COVID-19"? Does exposure count, and how should subjects who have been exposed but are asymptomatic be categorized in study data?
ANSWER: The template specifies that the study population for the asymptomatic claim should not include individuals with known exposure to COVID-19, as this would categorize them as suspected of having COVID-19. The distinction is between general screening populations (without any symptoms or specific reasons to suspect an active infection) and individuals symptomatic or suspected of having COVID due to exposure. However, there seems to be a need for further clarification on whether asymptomatic individuals known to have been exposed can count towards the asymptomatic group for the purpose of study enrollment. The FDA team suggests submitting detailed questions through the templates email for a precise answer and plans to provide clearer guidance regarding this situation.

QUESTION: For a high complexity CLIA laboratory that has validated an assay for whole genome sequencing of the COVID-19 virus and reports back to clinicians and patients, is an EUA request required to continue reporting these results?
ANSWER: Yes, for laboratories that have offered a test prior to November 15, 2021, without submitting an EUA request, the FDA expects an EUA submission within 60 days of the release of the updated guidance (i.e., 60 days from November 15, 2021). The FDA does not intend to object to the continued offering of the test while the EUA request is under review. For LDT labs that have developed, validated, and launched their tests without FDA interaction, they are encouraged to submit their existing documentation as is, without needing to fit into a specific FDA format. A cover letter explaining the organization of the submission may be helpful. However, if the laboratory shifts to reporting only to public health departments for public health monitoring purposes and not to clinicians or patients for clinical purposes, an EUA is not required.

QUESTION: Can you provide more details on the 45 and 60 day periods mentioned in the updated guidance regarding tests offered prior to an EUA submission, and can testing continue while an EUA review is pending?
ANSWER: The 45 and 60 day periods mentioned in the guidance are intended for tests that were being offered prior to an EUA submission under previous policies. These periods refer to the timeframe in which the FDA expects to receive an EUA request for those tests. The FDA will not object to these tests continuing to be offered during the FDA review process, provided they were offered for clinical use before November 15th, either under the policies of previous guidance or as a Laboratory Developed Test (LDT) following the HHS statement from August 2020. The specifics of this policy, including stipulations for continuing to offer the test while preparing for and during EUA review, are detailed in Section 4c and Appendix B of the November 15th guidance.

QUESTION: Is a pooling strategy a strong requirement for EUA, and is it necessary to provide just a brief description or clinical data for it, considering it may decrease sensitivity and prolong clinical studies?
ANSWER: Pooling is not a requirement for EUA; it's an option. Properly developed and validated pooling strategies that don't pool too many samples can be quite sensitive, especially for molecular tests. It can increase testing capacity and throughput. For kit developers, the quickest path to market is to validate individual sample tests first and then work on pooling as data allows. The details about pooling are well laid out in the template.

QUESTION: For home tests that lack validated external controls, how should a CLIA waived point-of-care lab go about validating an external control to use with these tests?
ANSWER: If the request comes from a developer at the time of authorization, labeling for point-of-care would typically be included. There may also be a post-market commitment to provide external controls for point-of-care sites. It is recommended for the developer to get the use of the external control added to the authorization if it is being validated for point-of-care use. Certificate of waiver sites under CLIA are required to perform a test according to the manufacturer's instructions for use, so using a control not in the instructions could cause issues. The developer should send this question to their previous reviewer for the authorization or the template's email box for a specific response.

QUESTION: Is the enrichment strategy still an option for the clinical study design of OTC antigen tests?
ANSWER: Yes, enrichment schemes are still an option. However, it's important to ensure that these schemes do not introduce bias that makes the candidate test appear better than it is. The FDA is open to reviewing study designs for enrichment through the template's email box or a pre-EUA submission. The goal is to ensure that the data collected accurately reflects the true performance of the test.
