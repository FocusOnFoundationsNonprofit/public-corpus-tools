QUESTION: Can you provide guidance on the process of establishing a primer set for LAMP and the dynamics of validation, inclusivity in silico analysis, and cross-reactivity, especially in the context of coordinating with other test developers to open source our test for screening asymptomatic populations?
ANSWER: We’ve seen that as a pathway that could be very successful. If you seek your own authorization for the test, you can give a right of reference for anyone else who wants to copy your test. This wouldn’t eliminate the requirement for them to get their own authorization if they're a kit developer. It also wouldn’t extend the EUA authorization to any lab that might copy that method. For example, the Yale saliva direct model has an instruction for use that is the basis of the kit, and they point to off-the-shelf products that users can purchase, such as the CDC’s primers and probes, which we previously authorized. There are two pathways: getting your own individual authorization for a kit and giving others the right of reference to copy it, or having a method authorized and then designating which lab can use your method and are therefore covered by your EUA.

QUESTION: If we get authorization for a test with a specific license or activation buffer and purification process, and we're working with another developer who is seeking authorization for a different purification amenable to automation, would they need to repeat the upfront parts of validation if we consolidated on the same primer set and LAMP master mix? Also, would they need to repeat the interfering substances validation?
ANSWER: At a high level, you’re close, but the devil's sometimes in the detail. Certain alterations to a test may require additional validation. If they’re adding something new that you didn’t do, then we would want to evaluate the data around that change. Developers can give each other right of references as they wish, and they can specify what right they have from their entire EUA. They can have limitations or open them up entirely. You can cross-reference each other’s assays to the extent that you want, and there would absolutely be synergy. If the components are all the same for the core test, then there would be synergy in reducing any duplication that isn’t recommended because the tests are basically the same in those functions.

QUESTION: Can point-of-care facilities use venous draw whole blood for antibody test kits, or are they limited to fingerstick whole blood specimens?
ANSWER: Point-of-care facilities, such as physician offices, are permitted to use both fingerstick and venous draw whole blood for antibody test kits. However, adding a step for plasma or serum would likely require a different process, and point-of-care facilities generally are not equipped for such procedures.

QUESTION: For retrospective studies of rapid antigen tests, what CT values should be considered low positives, and is it still recommended to use five fresh samples if the study uses dry fresh swabs that have been frozen?
ANSWER: The FDA recommends a minimum of five fresh positive samples as the device is intended to be used. While there is flexibility in completing fresh sample studies post-market, frozen swabs are not recommended for point-of-care sites due to impracticality. There is some evidence that freeze-thaw cycles may increase target availability and potentially improve assay performance, but this is not a standard practice.

QUESTION: What extent of the software validation is required for artificial intelligence needs in COVID-19 assays using a support vector machine for classification? And, should calibration set be included in a change plan for the AI algorithm calibration to each instrument?
ANSWER: For Emergency Use Authorizations (EUAs), the FDA typically does not require extensive software documentation as for a full authorization. The focus should be on the most critical components prior to authorization. The goal is to launch with no obvious errors in the software that could lead to false or inaccurate results, thus the bar for EUAs is lower. Recommendations for software validation depend on the software's intended use and its point of use. It's important to engage early with the FDA on the specific device rather than delaying submission due to extensive software validation concerns.

QUESTION: Is registration with the FDA the only requirement for saliva collection devices intended for COVID testing, or are there other requirements, particularly for devices not specifically designed for COVID but used for genetic or paternity testing? Additionally, does this change if the device is a simple empty tube without any preservatives or coatings?
ANSWER: Saliva collection devices for RNA preservation, including those not specifically for COVID but used in such tests, generally require FDA authorization beyond simple registration and listing. This could be in the form of clearance, approval, or an EUA. For an empty tube used for sample collection without preservatives or coatings, the responsibility falls on the test developer to ensure its suitability as part of their test. If a manufacturer intends to market sterile tubes specifically for saliva collection with RNA preservation, even if empty, performance with a representative assay must be demonstrated, and it must meet the regulatory requirements for an RNA preservation saliva collection device. Any claims about saliva collection for SARS-CoV-2 testing necessitate FDA review.

QUESTION: What performance features are required to obtain an over-the-counter (OTC) non-prescribed indication versus a prescription (Rx) non-lab use claimed indication? Specifically, is a 99% negative predictive value (NPA) for asymptomatic subjects the reason current non-lab use tests are prescription only? Additionally, for new multiplex products not previously cleared but including multiple analytes like SARS-CoV-2 and RSV, is it necessary to include validation for the non-SARS-CoV-2 analytes equivalent to a cleared 510k product?
ANSWER: The criteria for OTC non-prescribed indications do not strictly hinge on a 99% NPA for asymptomatic subjects. The FDA's recommendations are flexible, and lower specificity could be considered with appropriate mitigations such as labeling and training. Regarding new multiplex tests that include SARS-CoV-2 alongside other analytes like RSV, the FDA is open to evaluating these tests even if they are not based on previously cleared multiplex tests. For tests with new panels, additional data on the non-SARS-CoV-2 targets might be required. The FDA aims for a balance, requiring some post-market commitments for additional studies on these targets to align more closely with full authorization criteria while striving to be minimally burdensome in the pre-market phase up to the authorization decision. This approach indicates a strong encouragement for the development of new panels.

QUESTION: For the new at-home serology template, does a specimen collection device used with an LDT need to be a legally marketed collection device? Additionally, does the collection device require its own EUA, or can it be part of an EUA assay?
ANSWER: Home collection tests need to be authorized and are not considered Lab Developed Tests (LDTs) if intended for home collection. The specimen collection device must be legally marketed for the specific use it is being employed for. If a lab intends to use a collection device for SARS-CoV-2 testing, and no such collection devices are currently authorized for that purpose, they would need to seek authorization either as part of a test system or for the collection device as a standalone entity. The laboratory or entity using the device would then take regulatory responsibility for it.

QUESTION: If intending to use the Spectrum tube for LDT with home collection, is an EUA required for this setup?
ANSWER: The Spectrum tube, while authorized as a standalone collection device, is not authorized for home collection. For a device to be used in home collection, the entire kit needs authorization, which includes the distribution process, sample collection, packaging, and shipping instructions to ensure it reaches the lab in the appropriate timeframe. Thus, even if using the Spectrum tube, which is legally permissible for home collection, the entire home collection kit, including the LDT utilizing the Spectrum tube, needs to be authorized to cover all aspects of home collection.

QUESTION: What guidance can laboratorians expect from the FDA regarding assessing immune status in those receiving COVID-19 vaccination, including the timing for serologic response assessment, identifying specific antibodies, and determining the effectiveness of the vaccine through serologic tests?
ANSWER: The FDA, particularly the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER), has not linked diagnostic testing as part of vaccine approvals. While understanding immune response post-vaccination is a critical research area, the FDA has not authorized tests specifically for assessing the effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines. The FDA has authorized some neutralizing antibody tests and semi-quantitative tests that may aid research in this area. Any direct support for such indications would require comprehensive clinical studies. The FDA's current focus is to ensure the availability of accurate serology tests for their approved indications and to support ongoing research that may expand their uses.

QUESTION: For developing a rapid antigen test for home use, what are the differences in performance requirements for obtaining a prescription (Rx) versus a non-prescription (OTC) authorization?
ANSWER: For prescription (Rx) rapid antigen tests intended for home use, there may be age limitations based on the validation studies' age range, and there's no recommendation to validate for asymptomatic patients unless a specific claim for asymptomatic screening is made. For non-prescription (OTC) tests, there is an expectation of validation in asymptomatic populations due to the consumer's discretion in testing without a healthcare provider's involvement. OTC tests must demonstrate they can be accurately performed and interpreted by consumers, including on young individuals and asymptomatic persons. The FDA requires evidence of effectiveness in a small cohort of asymptomatic individuals (about ten) premarket, with potential additional post-market studies, and performance in children for OTC authorization.

QUESTION: Is there any hope for emergency use authorization (EUA) applications for serology tests submitted six months ago, considering the ongoing delay and lack of communication from the FDA?
ANSWER: Due to the overwhelming volume of applications, particularly for serology tests, and limited resources, the FDA is experiencing delays in processing EUA applications. However, recent efforts to address the backlog, including doubling the number of personnel working on COVID applications, have resulted in significant progress, with approximately 65 serology-related decisions made in the past two weeks. This surge in productivity indicates that the FDA is actively working to review and process submissions as efficiently as possible. The balance of resources between COVID-related and non-COVID applications remains a challenge, impacting the timeline for EUA reviews. Despite these efforts, the high volume of submissions means some delays are unavoidable.

ANSWER: For retrospective samples, while there's a general preference for CT values over 30 to qualify as "low positive," this may vary based on the comparator used. It's advised to consult with the FDA regarding the specific molecular comparator test to understand what constitutes a low positive in that context. For point of care rapid antigen tests, the requirement is for five positive fresh samples, not just five overall fresh samples, to assess adequate performance. Regarding low positives, engagement with the FDA for specific guidance based on the molecular comparator test is recommended.
